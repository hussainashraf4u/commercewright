wright /rīt/ noun

A maker or builder. Survives now only in compounds — wheelwright, shipwright, playwright. Archaic, and we know it. We kept it because the job description hasn't actually changed: someone draws the thing, someone builds the thing, and someone stays on afterward to keep it running. 011 — Where it breaks

Nothing fails at 10 a.m. on a Tuesday.

Three situations we get called into more than any others. If you recognise your own estate in one of them, that's the conversation to have.

  1. 01

    The certificate nobody renewed

    Friday, 6:40 p.m. A wildcard cert expires. Checkout starts returning TLS errors, the status page is green because it only pings the marketing site, and the person who originally requested that cert left in 2023. It takes ninety minutes to find out which registrar it even lives at.

    What it actually costs: one evening of revenue, and every customer who tried once.

  2. 02

    The bucket nobody owned

    A SOC 2 auditor asks who has access to production Terraform state. The state file turns out to be in an S3 bucket a contractor created in 2019 — no versioning, no owner tag, no MFA delete, and permissions granted to a group that still contains two former employees. Nobody did anything wrong. Nobody did anything, is the problem.

    This is the finding that delays your certification by a quarter.

  3. 03

    The upgrade nobody scheduled

    Your ERP hits end-of-support. The security patch you need only ships for the next major version, and that version breaks the four integrations someone wrote against an undocumented API. What should have been a maintenance window becomes a nine-month project with a hard deadline set by somebody else's release calendar.

    Deferred maintenance is a loan. The interest is paid in weekends.

022 — What we run

Four benches, one contract.

Most companies our size hire four vendors for this and spend the difference on coordinating them. Pick a bench to see how it wires together.

We move estates that can't afford to be down while they move.

  • AWS and Azure landing zones — accounts, guardrails, SSO, tagging that survives an audit
  • Terraform everywhere, reviewed in pull requests, no console cowboys
  • Colo and datacentre exits, including the servers nobody has logged into since 2021
  • Kubernetes where it earns its keep, plain VMs and managed services where it doesn't
  • FinOps — we cut Portway's monthly bill 57% and then showed them how to keep it there

Most of this work is integration, and integration is where the bodies are buried.

We write .NET, Node and Python, and we spend more time reading other people's code than writing our own. The usual job is a system that works but can't be changed safely: no tests, one deploy script, and an integration held together by a scheduled task on a machine under someone's desk.

077 — The awkward questions

Asked in almost every first call.

You take everything. Documentation, runbooks, Terraform, credentials, monitoring configuration — all of it lives in your repositories and your accounts from day one, not ours. Offboarding support is thirty days at no charge, and we'll sit on a call with whoever replaces us. We've done it. It's not fun, but a client who can't leave isn't a client, they're a hostage.

An engineer in Pune, where it is the middle of the working afternoon. That's the whole reason for the second office — nobody does their best incident response at 3 a.m. in their own time zone. Both offices are Commerce Wright employees on Commerce Wright payroll. We don't subcontract the night shift, and you can ask for the on-call rota.

Usually the opposite. In about two-thirds of our accounts there's an internal person who is very good and very outnumbered, spending their week on password resets instead of the work only they can do. We take the rota, the patching and the tickets. They get their job back. We'll say plainly if we think a role is redundant, but that has been rare.

You do, outright, on payment. Written into the MSA. The exception is our internal tooling — the landing-zone modules and monitoring templates we bring to every engagement — which stays ours and comes to you under a perpetual, transferable licence. So if you leave, it keeps working. We just don't hand a competitor our toolbox.

Median P1 first response across 2025 was 11 minutes against a contracted 15. The 95th percentile was 34 minutes, which is the number worth asking about, and it happened four times — twice during a regional provider outage when everyone paged at once. We hand you both numbers monthly. Any provider who quotes you only a median is showing you their good side.

Then wait, unless something is actually on fire. Read your notice clause first — most MSP agreements auto-renew with 90 days' notice, and people miss it. We're happy to run the Survey in parallel so you have documentation and a plan the day your notice lands. We won't help you break a contract, and we'd rather start a relationship without that being the first thing we did together.
